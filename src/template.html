    Why everything is measured against the trend line. Yields climb over the decades — new varieties, inputs, irrigation — so a raw comparison would call every recent year "good" and every early year "bad". Each place gets its own smoothed trend (gold line below); a year's effect is how far it landed above or below that line. That is what removes the technology trend and leaves the weather.

    ENSO on the globe

    Watch the Pacific warm and cool, and how cotton responded each year
    The box is Niño 3.4, a patch of the equatorial Pacific (5°N–5°S, 170°W–120°W).
    ONI = the 3-month average sea-temperature anomaly inside that box. ≥ +0.5 °C → El Niño, ≤ −0.5 °C → La Niña.
    Dots are cotton areas, coloured by that crop year's yield: green above trend, red below.
    Ocean colours are the observed sea-surface temperature anomaly for the chosen season (NOAA ERSST v5 vs 1991–2020): a red tongue along the equator is El Niño, a blue one La Niña. The dashed circles appear only during an event and show where that kind of event usually brings wetter or drier weather (NOAA/IRI typical-impact maps, schematic). Cotton areas inside a circle sit directly in the event's path. Use Nov–Jan to see an event at its peak (best for Brazil and Argentina); Jul–Sep matches the US and Indian growing season.

    Four quadrants of the globe

    Does the same event hit NW, NE, SW and SE cotton the same way?
    The equator and the prime meridian split the globe into four quadrants, and every mapped cotton area falls into one by its coordinates. Each quadrant's figure is the effect for the harvested-area-weighted yearly series of its areas (country by country, then combined by area), with a 90% range that reflects how many event years there are — neighbouring areas share the same weather, so they are not counted as independent evidence. Northern-hemisphere cotton (US, India) grows during the event's build-up; southern cotton (Brazil, Argentina) grows through its peak and decay — that timing, more than longitude, is what to watch.

    Continents

    North America · South America · Asia · Australia — does a continent's cotton move together?
    Each country belongs to one continent: North America = United States; South America = Brazil + Argentina; Asia = India + Pakistan + China; Australia = Australia (New South Wales and Queensland). A continent's figure is built like the quadrants: for each country, the harvested-area-weighted yearly series of its mapped areas gives a phase-minus-neutral effect with a Welch range; countries are then combined by cotton area. Countries marked β are exploratory (shorter or patchier official records); switch them off under ⚙ Settings to see the core four only. Each country uses its own growing window, so "La Niña years" are not the same calendar years everywhere.
